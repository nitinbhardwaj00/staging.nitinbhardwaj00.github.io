Lessons from building a SaaS company's analytics function from scratch, then watching it get stress-tested by investor due diligence and an M&A process.

At Shore, a Munich-based SaaS company that was later acquired by group.one, I built the analytics function from the ground up. There was no data team, no warehouse to speak of, and a lot of very reasonable questions nobody could answer quickly. Within that journey the company went through a fundraise and then a sale process — two moments where every number you've ever produced gets questioned by people who are paid to be sceptical.

Here's what I'd tell anyone starting the same job.

## 1. Your first customer is the CFO, not the dashboard

The temptation is to start with tooling: pick a warehouse, a BI tool, build pretty dashboards. The better start is to ask finance which three numbers they'd be most embarrassed to get wrong in front of investors — and make those bulletproof first. Everything else can wait.

## 2. Reconcile to the ledger, always

An analytics number that doesn't tie back to the accounting system will lose every argument, even when it's more useful. Build the bridge early: a monthly reconciliation from your revenue model to the books, with the differences explained. In due diligence, that bridge is worth more than any dashboard.

## 3. Due diligence is a data product

Investors and acquirers will ask for the same cuts again and again: cohorts, retention, unit economics, customer concentration, revenue by product and geography. If you can produce these from one governed model instead of a heroic spreadsheet sprint, you shorten the process and — just as important — you look like a company that knows itself.

## 4. Definitions decide valuations

During a transaction, small definitional choices — when churn is recognised, how expansion is netted, what counts as recurring — change headline metrics. Agree definitions *before* the data room opens, document them, and stick to them. Changing a definition mid-process looks like hiding something, even when it isn't.

## 5. Hire for translation, not just SQL

The most valuable people on an early analytics team are the ones who can sit with a sales leader, understand what they actually need, and come back with something that changes a decision. Technical skill is necessary; translation is what makes a team indispensable.

## 6. Design for being acquired

This one sounds cynical, but it's practical: an acquirer will want to integrate your data into theirs. Clean entity definitions, documented logic and a clear data lineage make that much easier — and make your team more valuable in the combined company.

## The contrarian view

There's a fair argument that early-stage companies over-invest in analytics. Before product-market fit, a founder with a spreadsheet and good instincts often beats a data team. A warehouse and a BI rollout can create the *feeling* of rigour without changing decisions. If I'm honest, some of what we built early was ahead of what the business could use.

The test I now use: if a piece of analytics work wouldn't change a decision this quarter or survive due diligence next year, it can probably wait.
